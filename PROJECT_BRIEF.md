## 1. One-Line Description

CyberPulse AI converts a filed cybercrime complaint into a ranked, explainable forecast of **where** and **when** the defrauded money is most likely to be withdrawn as cash — so that law enforcement and banks can act inside the golden hour instead of investigating after the trail has gone cold.

---

## 2. Executive Summary

When a citizen files a financial cybercrime complaint, the money has usually already begun moving. It is layered through a chain of mule accounts within minutes, and is typically converted to cash at an ATM or a cash-out point within a small number of hours. Once cash leaves the banking system, recovery probability collapses and the investigative trail effectively terminates.

Today the intervention model is *reactive*: a complaint is registered, an investigating officer requests transaction statements from banks, statements arrive over days, and by the time the mule chain is reconstructed the cash-out has already happened. The intelligence that would have been actionable — "this money will surface at a cluster of ATMs in this locality within the next two to four hours" — is produced, if at all, only in hindsight.

CyberPulse AI is a **decision-support platform** that closes this gap. It ingests a complaint together with the associated (synthetic, in this prototype) transaction and account signals, engineers behavioural, network, spatial and temporal features from them, and runs a gradient-boosted predictive pipeline to produce three coupled outputs: a **ranked list of likely cash-withdrawal hotspots**, an **expected withdrawal time window**, and a **calibrated risk score with a SHAP-derived explanation** of which factors drove the prediction. Those outputs are surfaced on a national GIS console, an interactive money-trail network graph, and a structured alert that can be dispatched to the relevant LEA unit, bank nodal officer and I4C desk — after which the alert is tracked through a full investigation lifecycle.

The system never asserts guilt. It ranks locations and time windows by risk, exposes the evidence behind each ranking, and leaves the decision with a human investigator. Every prediction is accompanied by its confidence, its contributing factors and its data provenance.

This prototype runs entirely on **synthetic, anonymised demonstration data** generated by a seeded, deterministic generator. It has no connection to NCRP, CFCFRMS, NPCI, or any bank core-banking system, and makes no claim of official endorsement or operational deployment.

## 3. Vision

A national cyber-fraud response capability in which every registered complaint automatically produces actionable, explainable, geo-temporal intelligence within seconds of filing — shifting Indian cybercrime enforcement from post-hoc investigation to pre-emptive interception.

## 4. Mission

To build a working, auditable, explainable predictive intelligence layer that sits between complaint registration and field response, and that a district cyber cell can operate without a data science team.

## 5. Problem

### 5.1 Problem statement (verbatim from PS 26184)

> "Development of a Predictive Analytics Framework for Cybercrime Complaints to Forecast Likely Cash Withdrawal Locations in Advance, Enabling Generation of Actionable Intelligence for Timely and Proactive Cybercrime Intervention."

### 5.2 Decomposition of the problem

| # | Sub-problem | Why it is hard |
|---|---|---|
| P-1 | The window for intervention is measured in hours, not days | Manual bank data requests take days; the operative window has closed by then |
| P-2 | The money trail is a graph, not a list | Funds fan out across layered mule accounts; a linear statement view hides the structure |
| P-3 | Cash-out location is not recorded anywhere in advance | It must be *inferred* from behavioural, network, spatial and temporal signals |
| P-4 | Signals are spread across complaint text, transaction logs, account history and ATM geography | No single system joins them |
| P-5 | Enforcement cannot act on an opaque score | Any output must be explainable, attributable and challengeable by an officer in court |
| P-6 | False positives have real cost | Deploying a team to a wrong locality wastes scarce resources and erodes trust |
| P-7 | Intelligence must reach the right recipient in a usable form | A score in a dashboard nobody is watching is not an intervention |

### 5.3 Consequence of the status quo

Recovery rates for cyber-financial fraud in India remain low relative to the volume of complaints, and the dominant reason is latency: intelligence arrives after the cash-out event rather than before it. The problem is not a shortage of data — it is a shortage of *timely, joined, explained* data.

## 8. Core Value Proposition

**For** a cyber cell investigating officer working a fresh financial fraud complaint,
**who** currently waits days for bank statements while the money is cashed out,
**CyberPulse AI** is a predictive intelligence console
**that** turns the complaint into a ranked, time-boxed, explained cash-out forecast in seconds,
**unlike** existing complaint-management and case-tracking systems
**which** record what happened but forecast nothing and produce no field-deployable intelligence.

---

## 9. Key Differentiators

1. **Prediction, not reporting.** The centre of the product is a forecast of a future event (a withdrawal), not a dashboard of past events.
2. **Coupled space and time.** A location without a time window is not actionable; the platform predicts both and presents them as a single intelligence unit.
3. **Explainability is a first-class output, not an add-on.** Every prediction ships with normalised SHAP factor contributions rendered in officer-readable language.
4. **The graph is the evidence.** The money-trail network view makes the inference auditable — an officer can see the account chain that produced the score.
5. **Ends in an action, not a number.** The workflow terminates in a dispatched alert with named recipients and a tracked investigation, closing the loop from complaint to intervention.
6. **Integrated single system.** The UI consumes real model output; no screen displays a hard-coded value that disagrees with the backend.
7. **Responsible by construction.** Synthetic data, neutral terminology, no automated accusation, explicit prototype labelling throughout.

## 13. Assumptions

Each assumption is recorded because the source specification did not settle it. Any change here must be reflected in `architecture/architecture-decisions.md`.

| ID | Assumption | Rationale | Risk if wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASM-01 | H3 resolution 8 (~0.74 km² per cell) is the right granularity for a "hotspot" | Matches a locality/market-block scale such as "Sector 18, Noida"; coarse enough to be actionable for a patrol team | Too coarse → not actionable; too fine → sparse labels. Resolution is configurable |
| ASM-02 | A 2-hour bin is the right temporal granularity | An enforcement team can realistically mobilise within a 2-hour window | Mis-sized window degrades perceived precision |
| ASM-03 | Cash-out occurs within 24 hours of the complaint timestamp in the prototype | Keeps the prediction horizon bounded and the label definition clean | Real distributions may be longer-tailed |
| ASM-04 | XGBoost on engineered tabular features outperforms deep models at this data scale | Standard result for tabular data; also far more explainable and reproducible | None material; LightGBM is a drop-in alternative |
| ASM-05 | Ranking candidate hotspots is a better formulation than regressing lat/lon | Ranking yields a top-k list, calibrated probabilities and clean hit-rate metrics | A regression formulation would lose the top-3 metric that judges understand |
| ASM-06 | Role switching without authentication is acceptable in the prototype | Explicitly permitted by the source specification (§29) | Must never be presented as production security |
| ASM-07 | PostGIS may be unavailable on the Neon free tier; H3 + Turf.js can carry all required geometry | Avoids a hard dependency on a database extension | Some queries become application-side; acceptable at prototype scale |
| ASM-08 | 500 complaints / 60,000 transactions is sufficient to train a demonstrable model | Enough signal for the planted latent patterns without slow training | Under-fitting; generator volumes are parameterised |
| ASM-09 | The ML service is called synchronously from Next.js route handlers | Simplest correct design at this scale; keeps the demo deterministic | Latency spikes on cold start; mitigated by a warm-up ping |
| ASM-10 | Estimated exposure = sum of unrecovered complaint amounts linked to the predicted hotspot | Needs a defined formula so UI and API cannot disagree | Ambiguity would break model/frontend consistency |

### 14.3 Sequencing dependencies

Synthetic data generation must complete before model training; model training must complete before the prediction API is meaningful; the prediction API must work before the dashboard is anything other than a shell. This ordering is enforced by the phase plan in `implementation/`.

## 15. Risks (summary)

The full register with probability, impact, mitigation, contingency, owner and detection method is in `project-management/risk-register.md`. The five that most threaten delivery:

| ID | Risk | Severity | Mitigation summary |
|---|---|---|---|
| RSK-01 | Synthetic data contains no learnable signal, so the model performs at chance | Critical | Plant explicit latent patterns; validate with a signal-check script before training; gate Phase 2 on ROC-AUC ≥ 0.85 |
| RSK-02 | ML service cold start makes the live judge demo stall | High | Warm-up ping on app load; 8s timeout with a cached last-good result for the demo complaint; `/demo` pre-warms on mount |
| RSK-03 | UI displays values that disagree with model output | High | Single typed contract in `packages/shared`; no hard-coded risk values anywhere; contract tests assert UI renders API output |
| RSK-04 | Map performance degrades with all layers enabled | Medium | Cluster ATM markers, cap heatmap points, lazy-load the map component, virtualise tables |
| RSK-05 | Scope creep into secondary pages before the core path works | High | Phase gating; Phases 1–3 must exit before Phases 7–8 begin |

### 16.1 Web application

| Layer | Choice | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Framework | Next.js (App Router) | Server components reduce client JS for data-heavy tables; route handlers remove the need for a separate Node API service; single deployable |
| Language | TypeScript (strict) | Shared types between API and UI are the primary defence against model/frontend divergence (RSK-03) |
| UI library | React 19 | Required by Next.js App Router |
| Styling | Tailwind CSS | Rapid, consistent, token-driven styling; suits a strict design system |
| Components | shadcn/ui | Accessible Radix primitives, owned in-repo, restyleable to a government palette |
| Icons | Lucide React | Consistent, neutral, no brand imagery |
| Charts | Recharts | Declarative, React-native, sufficient for the report set |
| Graph | React Flow (`@xyflow/react`) | Purpose-built for interactive node/edge graphs with custom nodes |
| Maps | MapLibre GL JS (primary), Leaflet (documented fallback) | Vector tiles, GPU rendering, no licence key; Leaflet retained as a low-risk fallback |
| Geometry | H3-js + Turf.js | Hex indexing and client-side spatial maths without a PostGIS dependency |
| Data fetching | TanStack Query | Caching, retry, loading/error state for client components |
| Validation | Zod | One schema shared by route handler validation and TypeScript types |

### 16.2 Data

| Layer | Choice | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Database | Neon PostgreSQL (serverless) | Postgres semantics, branchable, generous free tier, zero ops |
| ORM | Drizzle ORM | SQL-first, fully typed, migration tooling, no runtime query-builder overhead |
| Migrations | drizzle-kit | Versioned, reviewable SQL migrations checked into the repo |
| Spatial | H3 index columns (+ PostGIS if the instance supports it) | Portable; hex indexes make hotspot aggregation a plain `GROUP BY` |

### 16.3 ML service

| Layer | Choice | Justification |

## 17. Responsible-Use Statement

CyberPulse AI is **decision-support software for trained investigators**. It is not an adjudication system.

- All data in this prototype is **synthetic and anonymised**. No real complaint, account, transaction, ATM or person is represented.
- The system produces **risk indications about locations and time windows**, never determinations about individuals.
- Accounts are described with the neutral terms **"Mule Account"**, **"Suspicious Account"** and **"Risk Indicator"**. No screen labels a person a criminal.
- Every prediction exposes its **confidence** and its **contributing factors**, so an officer can challenge it.
- The platform makes **no claim** of MHA or I4C endorsement, of access to NCRP/CFCFRMS/bank systems, of guaranteed fraud prevention, or of guaranteed recovery.
- Every screen carries a persistent **"Sample / Synthetic Prototype Data"** indicator and the prototype disclaimer defined in `ux/ui-guidelines.md`.
